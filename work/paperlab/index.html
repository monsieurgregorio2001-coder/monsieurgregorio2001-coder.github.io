Case study

PaperLab

Type a sentence and watch it travel through one layer of the Transformer from Attention Is All You Need.

Proves AI theory and teaching

Live demoCode

  • React
  • TypeScript
  • Attention

Problem

Attention Is All You Need is the paper most AI work now rests on. It explains attention in a few lines of matrix notation. Most people who read it cannot picture what those lines do to a sentence.

The usual fixes are static diagrams or videos. They show one example someone else picked. You cannot type your own sentence and watch the numbers move.

I wanted a page where a student, or an interviewer, could do exactly that in a browser tab with nothing to install.

Approach

PaperLab runs one real Transformer layer on small seeded weights. It is not a trained model, and the page says so on screen. The math is the paper's math. The weights are toys.

You type a sentence of up to 16 tokens. Then you step through four stages: tokens, embeddings with positional encoding, attention, and the feed-forward block. Each stage shows the formula, a short plain explanation, a link to the paper section, and the actual matrices.

The model is sized to fit on screen: d_model = 16, 4 heads with d_k = 4, and d_ff = 32. Attention gets a heatmap per head, head toggles, a causal mask switch, and click-a-token to see what it attends to.

Architecture

Decisions

  • Chose a pure math module over math inside componentsbecause a function with no DOM can be tested against the paper's formulas, and the UI cannot drift from the numbers it shows.
  • Chose seeded toy weights over a trained modelbecause the page needs no download, no backend and no API key. The same sentence always gives the same output, so a test can pin it.
  • Chose HTML and Unicode formulas over KaTeXbecause a maths renderer would have been the largest thing in the bundle, for a handful of short formulas.
  • Chose a word-level tokenizer over subword BPEbecause whole words read clearly in a heatmap row. The page states the simplification next to the tokens.
  • Chose computed text colour over a fixed palette for heatmap cellsbecause cell values sit on every shade of the ramp, and a fixed colour fails contrast somewhere. ramp.ts picks it per cell for both themes.

Results

  • 19 testsnpm test runs 19 Vitest tests on the math: softmax rows sum to 1 within 1e-6, attention shapes, causal masking, positional encoding against the paper formula, layer norm, tokenizer edges and determinism.
  • 58 KBInitial JS and CSS, gzipped: 53.5 KB JS plus 4.4 KB CSS, from the production build. Self-hosted fonts add about 159 KB.
  • 96 / 100 / 100 / 100Lighthouse mobile preset on the build served by vite preview: performance, accessibility, best practices, SEO.
  • 0 violationsaxe-core in light and dark, on all four steps, at 360 and 1280 px, colour contrast included. No horizontal scroll at 360 px.

Measured on one machine against a local build, not the live site.

What I'd do next

  • Offer a small subword vocabulary beside the word tokenizer, to show why real models split words.
